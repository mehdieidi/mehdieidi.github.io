# Curatorial notes — 2026 research edition

## Source policy

The primary source remains Grady Booch, **“The History of Software Engineering,” IEEE Software, September/October 2018, pp. 108–114**. The complete supplied paper is bundled locally and every major narrative section remains represented in a physical gallery.

The museum now has three visibly distinct source modes:

1. **Booch paper** — galleries and exhibits that paraphrase the supplied essay.
2. **Research extension** — material beyond or parallel to the essay, supported by linked external historical/technical sources.
3. **Mixed** — the Grady Booch gallery, which combines statements in the paper with IEEE, OMG and Computer History Museum records.

“Why it matters” remains curatorial interpretation. It is not presented as a quotation or as a factual claim by Booch.

## Why the AI room is deliberately cautious

The 2025–2026 record is not reduced to a single claim about AI productivity. The museum juxtaposes:

- **SWE-bench (ICLR 2024):** a repository-level benchmark built from 2,294 real GitHub issues and pull requests across 12 Python repositories.
- **METR 2025 RCT:** 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 tasks, early-2025 AI tools; developers took 19% longer with AI in that particular setting.
- **DORA 2025:** nearly 5,000 technology professionals; AI use was near-universal among respondents, with perceived productivity gains and positive relationships with throughput/product performance, but a continuing negative relationship with delivery stability.
- **METR March 2026:** maintainer review of AI-generated SWE-bench Verified pull requests found automated passing did not imply mergeability; the authors warn against a naive translation from benchmark score to practical usefulness.

These studies measure different populations and outcomes. The museum therefore treats the effect of AI on software-engineering productivity as **context dependent and still empirically developing**, not as settled history.

## Grady Booch beyond the paper

The dedicated Booch gallery uses:

- IEEE Computer Society’s profile and 2016 Computer Pioneer Award citation: object-modeling work leading to UML; co-author of UML; founding member of the Agile Alliance and Hillside Group.
- OMG’s UML history: Booch and Jim Rumbaugh began unifying Booch/OMT in late 1994; Ivar Jacobson joined in 1995; UML 1.1 was adopted by OMG in 1997.
- Computer History Museum’s Software Preservation Group history: Booch’s 2002 “Preserving classic software products” email seeded the group.

## Explicit corrections retained from v3

### Whirlwind attribution
The printed essay says “Tom Kilburn and his work with Whirlwind.” The museum preserves Booch’s Whirlwind → SAGE argument but uses Jay Forrester as the Whirlwind project leader, consistent with Computer History Museum/MIT histories.

Verification: <https://computerhistory.org/blog/project-whirlwind/>

### UML standard date
The paper prints **1987** for OMG standardization. OMG records UML 1.1 as adopted in **1997**. The museum uses 1997.

Verification: <https://www.omg.org/uml/why-uml-is-important.htm>

### Standard spellings
The museum uses established spellings **Dina St Johnston** and **Erich Gamma** while preserving the contributions Booch assigns to them.

### Stack Overflow attribution
The essay compresses the history to Joel Spolsky; the collection adds Jeff Atwood as co-founder, following Stack Overflow’s company history.

### PDF extraction artifacts
Machine extraction can misread typography (for example `C++` as `C11`). The museum follows the rendered page and established spellings where clear.

## Visual policy

There are no invented historical illustrations. The wall imagery consists of real portraits, machine photographs, screenshots/artifact photographs or archival images. Text-only interpretive walls from v3 were converted to **hybrid photograph + text exhibits**, and every photograph has a provenance link in its dossier.

## v5 architectural and image-policy revision

The circulation architecture was revised after user testing showed that the former 9–10 metre enclosed connectors read as tunnels rather than doorways. Adjacent gallery centers are now normalized to a 21 metre step around 18 metre rooms, leaving only a shallow transition zone. Those zones are treated as full-height architectural vestibules framed by dark portal surrounds and bronze cornices, not as separate corridors.

Every forward exit now has a **Next Gallery** board physically mounted on the exit wall. It names the next room, gives its date range, and previews two of its core ideas before the visitor crosses the threshold. Current-gallery identity remains above the incoming doorway.

The room envelope was also enriched with museum-specific architectural details: recessed exhibit wall bays, wainscoting, chair rails, baseboards, crown trim, corner pilasters, floor borders, ceiling coffers, and picture lights. These are fixed architectural elements; the project continues to avoid floating decorative particles or historically meaningless 3D objects.

### Image uniqueness and offline provenance

The v5 media system replaces deliberate image reuse. All 70 exhibits have separate local media keys. Known archival images are pinned; previously duplicated uses are resolved through exhibit-specific Wikimedia Commons searches. The downloader reserves pinned file titles, tracks every selected Commons title, rejects duplicates, and prefers photographic JPEGs when appropriate while still allowing authentic screenshots, primary documents, and artifact images where those are the historically meaningful object.

The exact Commons metadata of the selected file is stored locally and loaded into the dossier, so attribution follows the image that is actually on the wall. After the first successful cache, wall imagery and the pinned Three.js module are local and can be served without network access.
